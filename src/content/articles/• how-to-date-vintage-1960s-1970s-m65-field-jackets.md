**Vintage 1960s and 1970s M-65 field jackets** are among the most collectible pieces of American military surplus, and dating one accurately comes down to reading its inside contract tag and zipper metal. Issued to U.S. troops beginning in 1965 during the Vietnam War, the OG-107 olive sateen M-65 inspired decades of civilian outerwear—including workwear staples covered in our [1990s Carhartt jacket dating guide](/articles/how-to-date-vintage-90s-carhartt-jackets). Because Alpha Industries and other military contractors produced millions of commercial replicas alongside genuine military-issue coats, knowing how to decode DSA numbers and zipper alloys separates a \$400 Vietnam-era original from a \$60 modern fashion jacket.

## How Do You Read the DSA Contract Code Inside a Vintage M-65 Jacket?

The white cotton specification label sewn inside the lower lining (or beneath the collar) gives you the exact fiscal year the U.S. government ordered the coat:

- **1965–1966 (`DSA-100-XXXX` Without Year):** The earliest Vietnam War contracts list `DSA-100-` followed by four or five digits without a two-digit year code. These ultra-rare **First Pattern** coats lack buttoned shoulder epaulets and feature silver aluminum zippers.
- **1967–1977 (`DSA 100-67` to `DSA 100-77`):** Beginning in **1967**, the Defense Supply Agency inserted the **two-digit fiscal year** directly after `DSA 100-`. For example, a tag stamped **`DSA 100-69-C-1452`** was contracted in **1969**, while **`DSA 100-74-C-0118`** dates to **1974**.
- **1978 and Later (`DLA 100-78`+):** In **1978**, the agency changed its name to the Defense Logistics Agency, updating the tag prefix from `DSA` to **`DLA 100-78`** and onward.
- **Spotting Civilian Alpha Industries Replicas:** Genuine military-issue jackets list a **13-digit NATO Stock Number (NSN)** (such as `8415-00-782-2939`) and a `DSA` or `DLA` contract code, but **never display a commercial brand logo**. If the inside tag features Alpha Industries’ famous **three-horizontal-bar logo** and lacks a DSA/DLA number, it was manufactured for civilian retail rather than military issue.

## Why Does Zipper Metal Matter When Dating a 1960s vs. 1970s M-65?

Even if the inside white contract tag has washed blank over fifty years, checking the **main front zipper and collar hood zipper** narrows down the decade immediately:

- **Silver Aluminum Zippers (1965–1971):** All true 1960s and early-1970s Vietnam War M-65 coats were built with **matte silver aluminum zippers** supplied by **Conmar**, **Talon**, **Crown**, or **Scovill**. Because aluminum glinted in sunlight and corroded in humid jungle conditions, the U.S. Army phased it out at the end of 1971.
- **Heavy Brass Zippers (1972–1980s):** Starting with **1972 contracts (`DSA 100-72`)**, the military switched to **heavy-duty brass zippers** (commonly stamped **Scovill**, **General**, or **Serval** on the pull tab—similar to the period hardware featured in our [1960s and 1970s Schott Perfecto guide](/articles/how-to-date-vintage-1960s-1970s-schott-perfecto-leather-jackets)).

## How Do You Check Fabric Weight, Hood Construction, and Button-In Liners?

Authentic 1960s and 1970s M-65 jackets were built from a dense **9-ounce 50% nylon / 50% cotton wind-resistant sateen** (`NYCO`) that softens and fades to a rich sage-olive patina:

- **Concealed Nylon Collar Hood:** Unzip the horizontal zipper along the back of the stand collar—an authentic M-65 stores a lightweight olive nylon emergency hood inside, connected to elastic shock cords.
- **Triangular Cuff Storm Flaps:** Look at the sleeve cuffs for fold-out triangular fabric extensions secured with Velcro tabs, originally designed to shield soldiers' hands in cold rain.
- **Internal Liner Buttons:** Authentic military M-65 jackets have rows of flat brown buttons sewn inside the lining designed to accept a quilted **M-65 button-in liner** (sold separately or bundled by collectors).

## How Do Vintage 1960s and 1970s M-65 Field Jackets Fit?

Because military field jackets were sized to fit *over* thick wool field shirts and quilted liners, their sizing runs noticeably generous compared to standard civilian jackets of the same era (such as the cropped cuts discussed in our [1960s–1970s Levi’s Type III Trucker guide](/articles/how-to-date-vintage-1960s-1970s-levis-type-3-trucker-jacket)):

- **Military Chest & Length Labeling:** Military tags list both a chest category (**Small**, **Medium**, **Large**) and a height length (**Short**, **Regular**, **Long**)—for example, **`MEDIUM - REGULAR`** (designed for a 37-to-41-inch chest).
- **Flat Measurements Guide:** A vintage **`Medium - Regular`** M-65 typically measures **22.5 to 23.5 inches pit-to-pit** with a **30-to-31-inch back length**, fitting like a comfortable modern Men’s Large. Most modern buyers size **down one size** (choosing a Military Small or Medium) for a tailored everyday fit.

## Quick 1960s–1970s M-65 Field Jacket Buying Checklist

1. **Read the Contract Prefix:** Confirm **`DSA-100-`** (1965–1977) or **`DLA 100-78`/`79`** on the white inside specification label—and make sure there is no commercial three-bar Alpha logo.
2. **Check the Zipper Alloy:** Silver aluminum zippers date the jacket to **1965–1971**; heavy brass zippers indicate **1972 or later**.
3. **Inspect the Shoulders:** Lack of buttoned shoulder epaulets confirms an ultra-rare **1965–1966 First Pattern** M-65.
4. **Verify Velcro & Drawcords:** Check that the waist and bottom hem cotton drawstrings are intact and that cuff Velcro tabs still grip cleanly.
5. **Size Down for Everyday Wear:** Remember that military sizing includes extra layering room—a tagged **Small-Regular** fits most modern Medium builds.
